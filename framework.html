FRAMEWORK & METHODOLOGY

How the UERA framework actually works

The Ultra Event Readiness & Risk Assessment isn't a quiz with a vibe-based score. Every checkpoint, every question, and every scoring rule was built for a specific reason. This is that reasoning, laid out plainly — written and maintained by me, Jeff Cady, the person who built it.

WHY IT EXISTS

Assess. Prioritize. Adjust.

UERA runs the same three-step process I use as a coach, and the same one I used for over a decade in cybersecurity risk assessment: figure out where the real risk actually is, rank it by what would hurt most, then build a concrete plan to close the gap — instead of a generic checklist that treats a taper-week reminder and a life-threatening gap with the same weight.

Most pre-race checklists are really just packing lists. UERA is built around a different question: if something goes wrong out there, what's the actual chain of events that gets you there, and did you already close that door? That's a risk-assessment framing, not a checklist framing, and it changes what gets asked and how it gets scored.

THE STRUCTURE

Ten checkpoints, not one score

A single overall number hides where the actual problem is. UERA scores ten separate checkpoints independently, then averages them — so "72% ready" always comes with a specific answer to "ready for what, and weak where."

THE SCORING

What the numbers actually mean

Every question has a set of answers ordered from lowest risk to highest. Your answer's position in that list becomes a fraction — the best answer scores 0, the worst scores 1, and answers in between land proportionally.

category_score = ROUND( (sum of every question's fraction ÷ number of scored questions) × 100 )
overall_score = ROUND( average of all 10 category scores )

tiers: 0–33 Low  |  34–66 Moderate  |  67–100 High

For anyone who prefers it written formally, here is the exact calculation the tool runs:

fq = iq ÷ mq iq = position of your answer (0 = lowest-risk option). mq = number of real answer options minus 1. Ck = ROUND( 100 × Σ fq ÷ Nk ) Nk = scored questions in checkpoint k. "Not applicable" answers count as fq = 0. Informational questions are left out. O = ROUND( Σ Ck ÷ 10 ) Every checkpoint carries the same weight (wk = 1/10). There are no event-specific weights or course-difficulty multipliers — what changes by discipline is which questions get asked, not how much each checkpoint counts.

Two deliberate exceptions to that simple math:

"Not applicable" doesn't get punished. If a question doesn't apply to your event — no overnight shelter needed, no on-course medical support to look up — selecting that option scores as 0 risk, not as a missing or wrong answer. A risk assessment shouldn't penalize you for a risk that isn't there.

One question is informational only. Whether you've progressed through shorter distances before your first ultra is useful context for a coach, but the science doesn't support treating it as a risk factor — UESCA's own certification material is explicit that this isn't a prerequisite for success. So that answer is shown, but excluded entirely from the score. A framework that scores things has to be honest about what it doesn't actually know is risky.

HOW TO ACTUALLY USE THE SCORE

This isn't fixed in one pass

In cybersecurity, nobody achieves "secure" and stops. You assess, you find the highest-priority gap, you close that one gap, and then you reassess — because fixing one thing can shift what the next-biggest risk actually is. A security program that never gets re-audited isn't secure, it's just untested. UERA is built to work the same way, on purpose.

Don't treat a Moderate or High score across all ten checkpoints as ten things to fix this week. Look at the Risk Skyline or the Readiness Radar, find the one checkpoint that's actually tallest or reddest, and work on just that one. Trying to fix everything at once is how people burn out two weeks into a training block and fix nothing.

Then reassess — retake the assessment and use Compare With a Previous Assessment to see, checkpoint by checkpoint, whether that specific thing actually moved. Not a feeling that you're "more ready," an actual before-and-after number. If it didn't move, that tells you something too: either the fix wasn't the right one, or it wasn't finished. Pick the next-highest checkpoint and repeat. That loop — assess, prioritize one thing, fix it, remeasure — is the whole framework, not just the scoring math.

None of that requires trusting me, or anyone, with your data. UERA runs entirely in your browser — there's no server collecting your answers, and Compare works the same way, reading both PDFs locally on your own device. It's private to you by default. You decide if anyone ever sees it, including me.

LIMITATIONS

What the score can't tell you

A framework that scores things should say plainly where its scoring is a judgment call. Here is where UERA's is.

  • Averaging can hide a single serious gap. Because the overall score is a plain average, one checkpoint in real trouble can be diluted by nine good ones. Nine checkpoints at 0 and one at 100 gives an overall score of 10 — "Low." Four checkpoints at 100 and six at 0 gives 40 — "Moderate." UERA has no override rule that forces the overall tier up when one checkpoint is severe. What it does instead is show every checkpoint's own score and tier, plus the Risk Skyline and Readiness Radar, so the weak point stays visible. Read the individual checkpoints first and the overall number second.
  • Equal weights are a design choice, not a measured result. Nothing here proves that Recovery Plan and Medical & Injury History deserve the same weight, or that heat acclimation should count for more in a desert 100-miler than in a cold-weather 50K. I chose equal weights because I had no defensible basis for any other set. Doing better would take outcome data I don't have.
  • The tier cutoffs are even thirds. Low, Moderate and High split the 0–100 range at 34 and 67. That is a readable convention, not a threshold derived from race outcomes or clinical research.
  • It is self-reported. UERA can't verify what you tell it. To limit wishful answers, the options describe what you have actually done rather than how ready you feel — for example, "multiple training hikes at or near my actual trip weight" versus "only hiked with a light daypack." That narrows the room for optimism, but it doesn't remove it.
  • It is not a medical assessment. The Medical & Injury History checkpoint captures what you report, not your actual health. UERA is a self-assessment aid for finding where your final prep needs attention. It isn't medical advice and doesn't replace a coach or a doctor.
DISCIPLINE-AWARE, NOT GENERIC

Built differently for every discipline

UERA covers four disciplines — ultramarathons, mountaineering, ski mountaineering (skimo), and long-distance hiking — and most of the assessment content actually changes depending on which one you select, not just the label.

Early in development, the Gear checkpoint asked every discipline the same questions: trekking poles, headlamp, shoe break-in. It worked fine for ultrarunners. For a mountaineer, it was actively wrong — nothing about ice axe competency, crampon fit, or crevasse rescue gear, the things that actually carry risk in that discipline. A mountaineer who was genuinely well-prepared would score as high-risk anyway, simply because the tool never asked about the gear that mattered to them.

That's now fixed across the assessment: Gear, Training, and the first-timer question sets all branch by discipline, built from each discipline's own literature rather than adapted from running. Mountaineering's training questions are grounded in Steve House and Scott Johnston's Training for the New Alpinism — aerobic base before intensity, loaded muscular endurance, periodized progression. Skimo's are grounded in the companion volume, Training for the Uphill Athlete, applied to that sport's actual weekly-hours structure. Neither is a relabeled version of the ultrarunning content.

HOW IT EVOLVES

A framework that keeps improving

Getting details like that right — what's actually a risk factor versus what's just useful context — comes from engaging with the source material directly, not assuming what "seems right." That same standard now runs on more than just my own review.

Coaches across ultrarunning, mountaineering, skimo, and long-distance hiking can flag a wrong link, a question that doesn't hold up, or a gap in what's covered — directly in the tool. Every change runs through an automated test suite before anything ships: every discipline, every score boundary, the PDF export, checked fresh every time, the same way a real risk assessment should be audited rather than trusted on faith.

What "sourced" does and doesn't mean here. Every question traces to a named source, but UERA has not been through formal validation. It has not had its content reviewed by an independent expert panel, its scores haven't been tested for repeatability, and they haven't been compared against actual race outcomes such as finishes, DNFs or medical-tent visits. Source-grounded is a lower bar than validated, and I'd rather say so than let the first imply the second.

If you're a researcher, a race director, or a coach who wants to help close that gap — reviewing question content, testing reliability, or tracking outcomes in a cohort — I'd like to hear from you at coach@jcady.org.
